# The Moving Parts

**Outcome:** the vocabulary the rest of the
guide uses, each piece tied to something
you can point at in the program or the file.

**Check:** slice anything — even the cube
in chapter 5 — and find the pieces below
in the output file's comments.

## The model

The input shape, as a skin of triangles.
An **STL** carries only the skin.
A **3MF** can carry more:
several objects, their positions,
and project settings —
which is why OrcaSlicer saves projects as 3MF.
The lab's test models were generated
by a small Python script
(`orcaslicer-lab/make_models.py`):
a 20 mm cube and two bars.
Ask the slicer about a model before slicing it:

```console
$ orca-slicer --info cube.stl
size_x = 20.000000
size_y = 20.000000
size_z = 20.000000
volume = 8000.000488 mm3
manifold = yes
number_of_facets = 12
```

Twelve triangles: two per face, six faces.
Volume 8000 mm³ is 20 × 20 × 20,
so the model is exactly the cube intended,
and **manifold = yes** means the skin
is watertight — a slicer can work out
what is inside and what is outside.

## The three presets

Every slice is decided by three presets,
chosen independently:

- **Machine** (called *printer* in the window):
  the physical printer's limits — bed size,
  nozzle diameter, maximum speeds,
  and the start and end instruction blocks
  wrapped around your part.
- **Process**: how this print is made —
  layer height, wall count, infill density
  and pattern, support rules, speeds.
  Process presets are usually named
  for their layer height, like
  `0.20mm Speed @MK3.5`.
- **Filament**: the plastic itself —
  nozzle and bed temperatures,
  cooling, flow, retraction behaviour.

In the window, the three sit as three
dropdowns in the left panel.
On the command line they are three files:

```text
--load-settings "machine.json;process.json"
--load-filaments "filament.json"
```

Presets **inherit**.
A machine preset names a shared base
(`inherits` in its JSON file),
the base names another,
and only the last file's own keys
are differences. That keeps dozens of
vendors' profiles maintainable.
It also means a preset file on its own
is not the whole story —
chapter 13 shows the trap this sets
for command-line users.

## The plate and the objects

Models sit on a virtual build **plate**.
One plate can hold several objects,
printed in sequence layer by layer —
layer 12 of every object, then layer 13.
Each object can, in principle, have
its own filament and even its own
process settings. In practice,
per-object filament assignment is
where the command line runs out,
as chapter 8 explains honestly.

## The G-code file

The output. Three regions, all readable:

1. A **header** of comments naming the
   slicer and version:
   `; generated by OrcaSlicer 2.4.2`.
2. The **body**: moves (`G1`), temperature
   commands (`M104`, `M140`), fan commands,
   and comment markers the slicer leaves
   as signposts — `;LAYER_CHANGE` at each
   new layer, `;TYPE:Outer wall`,
   `;TYPE:Sparse infill` and friends
   around each kind of path.
3. A **config block and footer**:
   every setting that produced the file,
   then the slicer's own summary —
   total layers, filament used,
   estimated time.

Those signposts are why chapter 6 works:
the file testifies about itself.
